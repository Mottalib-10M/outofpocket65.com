Does Medicare cover it?

Does Medicare cover a podiatrist?

Yes, when the visit treats a foot injury or disease, under Part B. The estimator prices a podiatrist visit, or nail care in the limited cases Medicare pays for it, with your area.

How this is calculated

Medicare Part B covers foot exams and treatment by a podiatrist, a foot doctor, when you need medically necessary treatment for a foot injury or disease, such as hammer toe, bunion deformities or heel spurs. After the $283 Part B deductible you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount, and in a hospital outpatient setting you also pay a hospital copayment. A podiatrist office visit for a foot problem is approved at $95 at national rates in 2026, so it costs you $19 once the deductible is met, or $95 as the first Part B bill of the year. Routine foot care is usually not covered: cutting or removing corns and calluses, trimming, cutting or clipping nails, and hygienic or preventive maintenance such as cleaning and soaking the feet. In most cases you pay all of it. In very limited circumstances Medicare does cover routine foot care, and then you pay 20% after the deductible: a nail care visit approved at $24 leaves you $5. Ask the podiatrist which part of the visit will be billed to Medicare.

Medical foot care versus routine foot care

The line Medicare draws is between treatment and upkeep. Exams and treatment for foot injuries or diseases are covered as medically necessary services: a painful bunion, a hammer toe, a heel spur, an injury that needs care. Routine foot care is upkeep: corns and calluses cut or removed, nails trimmed, cut or clipped, and hygienic or preventive maintenance like cleaning and soaking your feet. Medicare does not usually cover it.

Your area and your podiatrist

A podiatrist bills under the physician fee schedule, so the approved amount follows your price area: $108 for a visit in Manhattan, $88 in Alabama. A podiatrist who does not accept assignment is approved 95% of the fee and may bill up to the limiting charge: on our sample visit you pay $32 instead of $19. The medicare.gov page on assignment explains how to check.

Medigap and Medicare Advantage

Medigap pays its share of covered foot care. Plan G pays the 20% and the excess charge, leaving you $0 on a visit once the deductible is met. Plan N pays the 20% but may leave a copayment of up to $20 for some office visits ($19 here) and does not pay the excess charge. Routine foot care that Medicare does not cover is not paid by any Medigap letter. A Medicare Advantage plan sets its own copay for a specialist visit; enter it in the estimator.

A year of foot care

Costs add up over visits. Four podiatrist visits for a heel spur in a year cost you $76 at national rates once the deductible is met, or $303 if they are your first Part B bills of the year. In a hospital outpatient clinic, a hospital copayment is added to each visit. Original Medicare has no yearly cap on the 20%, so a long course of treatment is where a Medigap policy makes the largest difference.

Before the visit

Tell the office why you are coming: a foot injury or disease, or routine care. Ask whether the podiatrist accepts assignment, and which services of the visit will be billed to Medicare and which to you. If you get routine nail care at the same visit as a covered treatment, the bill can show a covered line at 20% and a line you pay in full. Doctors may recommend services Medicare does not cover; ask why, and what Medicare will actually pay. Coverage rules come from the medicare.gov foot care page.

Questions people on Medicare ask

Does Medicare pay for toenail trimming?

Usually not. Trimming, cutting or clipping nails is routine foot care, which Medicare does not usually cover; in most cases you pay the whole price. In very limited circumstances Medicare covers it, and you then pay 20% of the approved amount after the deductible, $5 per visit at national rates. Ask the podiatrist whether your condition qualifies before the visit.

Is bunion or heel spur treatment covered?

Yes. Part B covers podiatrist exams and treatment that are medically necessary for foot injuries or diseases, and medicare.gov names hammer toe, bunion deformities and heel spurs as examples. You pay 20% after the deductible. A podiatrist office visit for such a problem costs you $19 at national rates once the deductible is met; surgery or imaging, if needed, is billed separately.

Can a podiatrist charge more than the Medicare-approved amount?

Only a podiatrist who does not accept assignment. Medicare then approves 95% of the fee and the podiatrist may bill up to the limiting charge, 15% above that amount. On a podiatrist visit that means up to $14 more, which Medigap Plans F and G pay. Check whether the office accepts assignment when you book.
