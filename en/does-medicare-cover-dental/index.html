Does Medicare cover it?

Does Medicare cover dental care?

In most cases, no: routine dental work is paid in full by you under Original Medicare. A short list of dental services linked to a covered medical treatment is the exception. Enter a dental bill in the tool to see which rule applies and what is left for you.

How this is calculated

Original Medicare does not cover routine dental care: cleanings, fillings, tooth extractions, dentures and implants are paid by you in full, and Medigap pays nothing toward them because there is no Medicare-approved amount to share. Medicare steps in only when the dental work is tied to a medical treatment it covers and the success of that treatment depends on it. The cases named by Medicare are an oral exam and dental treatment before a heart valve replacement or a bone marrow, organ or kidney transplant; a procedure such as an extraction to clear a mouth infection before cancer treatment like chemotherapy; treatment of a complication during head and neck cancer treatment; and dental or oral exams, plus treatment of an oral infection, before and during dialysis for end-stage renal disease. Covered outpatient dental services cost 20% of the approved amount after the $283 Part B deductible, plus a facility copayment in a hospital outpatient setting. When you are admitted to a hospital for the dental procedure, Part A applies: the $1,736 deductible in 2026 covers days 1 to 60 of the benefit period, so a three-day stay leaves you $1,736 for the hospital. Some Medicare Advantage plans add a dental benefit.

The general rule: routine dental work is yours to pay

Original Medicare was not built to pay dentists. Its own list of excluded items names routine cleanings, fillings, tooth extractions, dentures and implants, and Medicare says you pay all costs for these. There is no approved amount, no 20% share and no deductible credit: the dentist's price is the price. A Medigap policy does not change that, since it only pays part of costs Medicare has approved first.

This holds whatever your age or the reason your teeth need work. Even a painful abscess treated in a dental office is, as a rule, a private expense under Original Medicare. The exceptions below are narrow and depend on a link with another treatment, not on how urgent the dental problem feels.

Exceptions tied to a covered medical treatment

Medicare may pay for specific inpatient or outpatient dental services when they are directly related to a medical treatment it covers and that treatment's success depends on them. The cases Medicare spells out are these:

  • an oral exam and dental treatment before a heart valve replacement, or before a bone marrow, organ or kidney transplant;
  • a procedure such as a tooth extraction to treat a mouth infection before cancer treatment, chemotherapy for example;
  • treatment of a complication that appears during head and neck cancer treatment;
  • dental or oral exams before and during Medicare-covered dialysis for end-stage renal disease, and medically necessary tests and treatment to remove an oral or dental infection over the same period.

Medicare's list of non-covered services also mentions heart valve repair among the treatments that can bring related dental care under coverage. In each case the paperwork matters: the dental service has to be documented as part of the medical treatment plan, so ask the treating doctor and the dentist to coordinate before the work is done.

What a covered dental service costs you

When the linked dental service is done as an outpatient, it falls under Part B. You pay the $283 deductible if it is not yet met in 2026, then 20% of the Medicare-approved amount. In a hospital outpatient department or another facility setting, the facility also charges you a copayment. The mini tool above applies that rule to the bill you enter and shows what changes when the work is linked to a covered treatment.

Sometimes the dental procedure requires a hospital admission, because of your underlying condition or because the procedure itself is severe. Part A then covers the hospital, and the 2026 amounts apply per benefit period.

Dental benefits through other coverage

Medicare Advantage plans may offer extra benefits Original Medicare does not include, dental care among them, and so may Medicare Cost plans and PACE. Other health coverage you hold can also help. Each plan decides what it pays, how often, and through which dentists, so the details are in the plan's own documents. If your plan gives a yearly dental allowance, type it in the mini tool with the bill: the tool shows what remains after the allowance is used.

Questions to settle before the appointment

Is the dental work needed for a treatment Medicare covers, and will the treating doctor say so in writing? Will it be done in a dental office, a hospital outpatient department or during an inpatient stay? Has the Part B deductible already been met this year? The first answer decides whether Medicare pays anything at all; the other two decide how much of the bill stays with you. For routine care with no medical link, the useful question is simpler: what is the full price, and what does it include?

Questions people on Medicare ask

Will Medicare pay for a tooth extraction before chemotherapy?

It can. Medicare lists a procedure such as a tooth extraction to treat a mouth infection before cancer treatment, chemotherapy for example, among the dental services it may cover, because the medical treatment depends on it. Done as an outpatient, you pay 20% of the approved amount after the Part B deductible. An extraction with no link to a covered treatment stays at your full cost.

Are dentures or dental implants ever covered by Original Medicare?

Medicare names dentures and implants among the items it does not cover in most cases, alongside cleanings, fillings and extractions. Its exceptions concern exams and treatments needed for a covered medical treatment to succeed, not the replacement of missing teeth. If you want coverage for dentures, look at the dental benefit of a Medicare Advantage plan or at other coverage you may have.

What does a hospital stay for dental surgery cost with Medigap?

When the stay is covered by Part A, the $1,736 deductible is the main cost for a short admission. Medigap Plan G pays it in full, which brings a three-day stay to $0 for the hospital part. Plan K pays half of the Part A deductible, so the same stay leaves $868. The dentist or surgeon also bills Part B, which Medigap shares as usual.

Does dialysis change what Medicare pays at the dentist?

Yes, for people with end-stage renal disease. Medicare may cover dental or oral exams before and while you receive Medicare-covered dialysis, and medically necessary tests and treatments to remove an oral or dental infection over the same period. These count as Part B services: 20% of the approved amount after the deductible. Routine care such as a cleaning stays outside the rule.
